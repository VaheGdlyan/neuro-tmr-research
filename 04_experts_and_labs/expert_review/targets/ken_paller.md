## Why This Expert Matters

Ken Paller is one of the most relevant reviewers for Neuro-TMR because his work spans both the scientific foundations of Targeted Memory Reactivation and attempts to move TMR beyond manually controlled laboratory experiments.

His laboratory contributed to foundational auditory TMR research showing that individual memories can be selectively strengthened by presenting associated sounds during sleep. He later co-developed major conceptual work on TMR and co-authored the first large meta-analysis evaluating TMR effects across the field.

Particularly important for Neuro-TMR, Paller also co-developed **SleepStim**, an automated home TMR system that used smartwatch-derived movement and heart-rate information to estimate deep sleep and automatically trigger auditory cues.

This makes him valuable not only for evaluating whether our interpretation of TMR evidence is correct, but also for directly challenging our decision to treat EEG-guided sleep-state detection as the most defensible first-generation pathway.

## Relevant Research

### Foundational Work

- **Rudoy, Voss, Westerberg & Paller (2009) — "Strengthening Individual Memories by Reactivating Them During Sleep" — Science**
  - One of the foundational auditory TMR demonstrations. Sounds associated with individual spatial memories during learning were replayed during sleep, producing selective post-sleep memory benefits.
  - Directly relevant to the fundamental TMR mechanism underlying Neuro-TMR.
  - DOI: https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1179013

- **Oudiette & Paller (2013) — "Upgrading the Sleeping Brain with Targeted Memory Reactivation" — Trends in Cognitive Sciences**
  - Important conceptual paper defining the potential and limitations of manipulating memory reactivation during sleep.
  - Highly relevant to our translation from experimental TMR toward neurotechnology.
  - DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tics.2013.01.006

- **Hu, Cheng, Chiu & Paller (2020) — "Promoting Memory Consolidation During Sleep: A Meta-Analysis of Targeted Memory Reactivation" — Psychological Bulletin**
  - First major quantitative synthesis of the TMR literature, covering 91 experiments and 212 effect sizes.
  - Reported an overall small-to-moderate memory benefit and found the clearest benefits for cueing during NREM stages N2 and N3.
  - This meta-analysis is one of the central evidence sources used in Phase I.
  - DOI: https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000223

- **Whitmore, Harris, Kovach & Paller (2022) — "Improving Memory via Automated Targeted Memory Reactivation During Sleep" — Journal of Sleep Research**
  - Developed **SleepStim**, an automated home TMR system using smartwatch movement and heart-rate data together with a smartphone for auditory cue delivery.
  - A machine-learning model estimated deep sleep and automatically triggered TMR.
  - The work showed that automated home TMR was feasible, while also demonstrating that stimulus intensity and avoiding sleep disruption were critical.
  - Particularly important because this represents a serious challenge/comparison point for our EEG-first reference pathway.
  - DOI: https://doi.org/10.1111/jsr.13731

### Recent Work

- **Paller (2024) — "Recurring Memory Reactivation: The Offline Component of Learning" — Neuropsychologia**
  - Develops the broader idea that recurring offline memory reactivation is a central component of learning and memory transformation.
  - Useful for evaluating whether our mechanistic interpretation of sleep-based reactivation is too narrow or simplified.
  - DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuropsychologia.2024.108840

- **Whitmore, Yamazaki & Paller (2024) — "Targeted Memory Reactivation with Sleep Disruption Does Not Weaken Week-Old Memories" — npj Science of Learning**
  - Investigates how the consequences of TMR interact with sleep disruption and memory age.
  - Shows that the outcome of reactivation depends on characteristics of the memory and the physiological context in which reactivation occurs.
  - Important for questioning simple assumptions that cue delivery has uniform effects.
  - DOI: https://doi.org/10.1038/s41539-024-00276-0

- **Subramanian, Zelano, Paller & Schechtman (2026) — "Concurrent Presentation of Memory-Related Odours and Sounds Nullified Sleep Reactivation Benefits" — Journal of Sleep Research**
  - Found that combining memory-related auditory and olfactory cues did not simply produce stronger TMR benefits and instead nullified the expected reactivation benefit.
  - Relevant as evidence that cue design and interactions between cues can produce non-obvious outcomes.
  - DOI: https://doi.org/10.1111/jsr.70314

## Relevance to Neuro-TMR

Paller is unusually relevant because he can evaluate several layers of the Neuro-TMR reasoning simultaneously:

1. **TMR efficacy** — whether Phase I represents the strength, heterogeneity, and limitations of the behavioral evidence correctly.
2. **Mechanistic interpretation** — whether memory reactivation and sleep-dependent consolidation are being translated appropriately into intervention requirements.
3. **Stage-aware cueing** — whether targeting broad NREM states is a reasonable first-generation simplification or whether oscillatory/event-level information should play a larger role.
4. **Automated home TMR** — his SleepStim work provides direct experience with automated cue delivery outside a traditional manually supervised PSG experiment.
5. **EEG-first reasoning** — SleepStim used peripheral wearable signals rather than EEG, giving him an especially valuable perspective for challenging our argument that EEG should be the first-generation sensing pathway.
6. **Arousal / sleep disruption** — his work shows that cue intensity and sleep disruption can materially alter the effect of memory reactivation.

His current scientific direction also remains highly relevant to translation. In his 2025 George A. Miller Prize lecture, he discussed possible approaches for amplifying sleep-related cognitive benefits, including strategies using wearable technology in the home.

## Phase-I Claims They Can Challenge

- **The overall TMR evidence is sufficiently consistent to justify moving from literature synthesis toward focused technical validation.**
  - Paller can evaluate whether our interpretation of the TMR evidence, including the Hu et al. meta-analysis, is appropriately conservative.

- **EEG-guided sensing is the most defensible first-generation pathway because it provides more direct access to the cortical physiology underlying TMR than peripheral sensing.**
  - His SleepStim work demonstrated automated home TMR using smartwatch movement and heart-rate data, making him particularly well positioned to challenge whether our EEG-first preference is actually justified by current evidence.

- **Stage-aware NREM targeting is an adequate first-generation control strategy, with more precise phase/event-aware stimulation left for later generations.**
  - His work and collaborations include evidence relating TMR benefits to slow-oscillation phase and other sleep physiology, so he can assess whether our simplification is reasonable or premature.

- **Avoiding arousal can be treated primarily as a system-control requirement.**
  - His recent work suggests that sleep disruption, cue intensity, memory age, and cue interactions may fundamentally alter the behavioral consequences of reactivation.

## Questions for This Expert

1. **Your group demonstrated automated home TMR with SleepStim using smartwatch-derived movement and heart-rate signals rather than EEG. Based on the evidence available today, do you think an EEG-guided system is scientifically justified as the most defensible first-generation pathway, or does successful peripheral-sensor-guided TMR suggest that this distinction is less important than we assume?**

2. **For an initial closed-loop TMR system, do you think detecting an appropriate NREM sleep state is a reasonable starting point, or has the evidence on slow-oscillation phase, spindles, and other electrophysiological factors become strong enough that finer temporal targeting should be considered a fundamental requirement?**

3. **Before serious engineering of an automated TMR system, what do you consider the most important unresolved scientific issue — for example cue intensity/arousal, individual variability, repeated-night effects, memory characteristics, physiological timing, or another factor that our Phase-I synthesis may be underestimating?**
