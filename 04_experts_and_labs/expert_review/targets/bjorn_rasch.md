## Why This Expert Matters

Björn Rasch is one of the foundational researchers in human Targeted Memory Reactivation and remains actively involved in research on sleep, memory, sensory stimulation, and real-time manipulation of sleep.

His 2007 Science study demonstrated that re-presenting an odor associated with prior learning during slow-wave sleep could selectively improve declarative memory and reactivate the hippocampus.

His later work is especially relevant to Neuro-TMR because it extends beyond basic TMR efficacy into questions such as:

- vocabulary reactivation;
- prior encoding and learning difficulty;
- home-based unsupervised TMR;
- sleep disturbance and habituation;
- real-time sleep stimulation;
- alternative cue-delivery modalities;
- practical translation of sleep manipulation outside tightly controlled laboratory paradigms.

He can therefore challenge both the scientific assumptions underlying TMR and several important translational decisions in the Phase-I reference pathway.

## Relevant Research

### Foundational Work

- **Rasch et al. (2007) — "Odor Cues During Slow-Wave Sleep Prompt Declarative Memory Consolidation" — Science**
  - Foundational human demonstration showing that re-exposure to a learning-associated odor during slow-wave sleep improved hippocampus-dependent declarative memory.
  - The effect was not observed when the odor was presented during REM sleep or wakefulness, or when it had not been associated with prior learning.
  - Directly relevant to sleep-state dependency and the requirement for prior cue-memory association.
  - DOI: https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1138581

- **Schreiner & Rasch (2015) — "Boosting Vocabulary Learning by Verbal Cueing During Sleep" — Cerebral Cortex**
  - Demonstrated benefits of replaying previously learned vocabulary during sleep.
  - Highly relevant to the Neuro-TMR project's interest in structured learning tasks such as vocabulary acquisition.

- **Göldi & Rasch (2019) — "Effects of Targeted Memory Reactivation During Sleep at Home Depend on Sleep Disturbances and Habituation" — npj Science of Learning**
  - Tested unsupervised vocabulary TMR across several nights in participants' homes.
  - TMR benefits depended strongly on whether cueing disturbed sleep, and the authors highlighted habituation, sleep monitoring, and avoidance of cue-induced awakenings as important requirements for real-world TMR.
  - Particularly relevant to home translation and arousal-monitoring requirements.
  - DOI: https://doi.org/10.1038/s41539-019-0044-2

### Recent Work

- **Wick & Rasch (2023) — "Targeted Memory Reactivation During Slow-Wave Sleep vs. Sleep Stage N2: No Significant Differences in a Vocabulary Task" — Learning & Memory**
  - Compared TMR during SWS and N2 and found no significant difference in vocabulary-memory benefit between the two conditions.
  - Highly relevant to the Phase-I assumption that N3/SWS should be the preferred first-generation target.
  - This paper makes Rasch particularly valuable for challenging an overly strong N3-first interpretation.

- **Klaassen & Rasch (2024) — "Difficulty in Artificial Word Learning Impacts Targeted Memory Reactivation and Its Underlying Neural Signatures" — eLife**
  - Found that TMR benefited easier-to-learn artificial words but not the more difficult items.
  - Effective TMR was associated with differences in spindle-band activity nested within slow-wave up-states.
  - Important because it shows that TMR efficacy depends not only on detecting the correct sleep state but also on properties of prior encoding and memory strength.
  - DOI: https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.90930

- **Fabbri et al. (2025) — "Music Cue During Slow Wave Sleep Improves Visuospatial Memory Consolidation" — Journal of Sleep Research**
  - Found better visuospatial memory after music cueing during SWS compared with REM cueing and a control condition.
  - Relevant to cue modality, sleep-stage specificity, and practical sensory stimulation.
  - DOI: https://doi.org/10.1111/jsr.14372

- **Krugliakova et al. (2026) — "Hacking the Functions of Sleep: Noninvasive Approaches to Stimulate Sleep Neurophysiology" — Physiological Reviews**
  - Rasch co-authored this major review of noninvasive approaches for manipulating sleep physiology.
  - The review emphasizes the complexity of sleep macrostructure and microstructure and the importance of state-dependent, often real-time stimulation strategies.
  - Relevant to the broader translation from sleep science toward closed-loop intervention.
  - DOI: https://doi.org/10.1152/physrev.00007.2025

## Relevance to Neuro-TMR

Rasch is relevant to several major Neuro-TMR decisions simultaneously.

### TMR Foundations

His 2007 work directly underlies the basic idea that an external cue associated with learning can bias later memory processing during sleep.

### Sleep-Stage Targeting

His research provides both support and challenge to a simple SWS-first model.

The foundational odor study demonstrated SWS-specific effects relative to REM and wakefulness, while later work comparing N2 and SWS found no significant advantage for SWS in a vocabulary task.

He can therefore help us avoid turning "TMR works during NREM" into the stronger and potentially unjustified statement that "N3 is always the optimal target."

### Encoding Quality

His recent work shows that properties of pre-sleep learning can determine whether TMR is effective.

This is important because a technical system cannot compensate automatically for memories that were insufficiently or differently encoded before sleep.

### Home Translation and Arousal

His home TMR research showed that sleep disturbance and habituation can strongly influence outcomes.

This makes him valuable for evaluating whether cue intensity, arousal monitoring, habituation, and participant adaptation should be treated as central system requirements.

### Closed-Loop Translation

His involvement in work on real-time sleep stimulation gives him a useful perspective on whether the Phase-I closed-loop framing is scientifically justified and which control variables are most important.

## Phase-I Claims They Can Challenge

- **N3/SWS should be the preferred first-generation cueing stage.**
  - Rasch's foundational work supports SWS-dependent TMR, but his later N2-vs-SWS study indicates that SWS may not always provide a larger behavioral benefit.

- **Correct sleep-stage detection is the primary determinant of successful TMR.**
  - His recent encoding-difficulty work suggests that pre-sleep learning quality and memory characteristics can strongly constrain TMR efficacy even when cueing occurs during appropriate NREM sleep.

- **Arousal avoidance can be treated as a secondary control requirement.**
  - His home-based work indicates that sleep disturbance and habituation may determine whether real-world TMR helps, does nothing, or potentially harms memory performance.

- **EEG-first is necessarily the most defensible first-generation sensing choice.**
  - His work supports EEG as valuable for examining sleep physiology, but his broader translational research does not itself establish that EEG-guided control is behaviorally superior to simpler sensing strategies. He can help assess whether our EEG-first reasoning is sufficiently evidence-based.

- **Auditory cueing is the obvious practical modality for V1.**
  - His work across odor, verbal cues, music, and olfactory wearable approaches gives him an unusually broad perspective on cue modality and practical deployment.

## Questions for This Expert

1. **Your work spans the original SWS odor-cue study and later research finding no significant difference between TMR during SWS and N2 in a vocabulary task. Based on the current evidence, how strongly should a first-generation TMR system prioritize N3/SWS rather than broader NREM cueing?**

2. **Your recent work shows that learning difficulty and prior encoding can substantially change whether TMR produces a benefit. For a practical TMR system, how important is it to measure or calibrate pre-sleep memory strength before deciding which memories should be cued?**

3. **Your home-based TMR work highlighted sleep disruption and habituation as major determinants of outcome. Before attempting engineering of an automated home system, which control requirement would you consider most important to validate first: sleep-state detection, cue intensity/arousal monitoring, encoding quality, habituation, or another factor?**
