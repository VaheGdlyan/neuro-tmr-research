## Why This Expert Matters

Eitan Schechtman is particularly valuable because his recent work investigates limitations and complexities that emerge when the simple laboratory model of TMR is extended to interacting memories, contexts, multiple cues, and the temporal dynamics of reactivation.

His research asks questions that are highly relevant to Neuro-TMR:

- Does cueing one memory reactivate only that memory?
- How does learned context influence what is reactivated?
- Can several memories be reactivated together?
- When do multiple sensory cues interfere?
- How long must a reactivation event last?
- Can neural data reveal the content of memory processing rather than only the sleeper's physiological state?

His laboratory also combines TMR with EEG, neuroimaging, computational methods, and machine-learning approaches for decoding memory-related neural content.

This makes him valuable for challenging whether the Neuro-TMR pathway is currently too focused on:

> sleep-state detection → cue delivery

without sufficiently considering what happens to the memory representation after the cue is delivered.

## Relevant Research

### Foundational Work

- **Schechtman et al. (2020) — "Targeted memory reactivation during sleep boosts intentional forgetting of spatial locations" — Scientific Reports**
  - Demonstrated that TMR can influence intentionally directed memory processing rather than simply strengthen all cued information.
  - Relevant to the broader question of what kind of memory transformation TMR can produce.
  - DOI: https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-020-59019-x

- **Schechtman et al. (2021) — "Multiple memories can be simultaneously reactivated during sleep as effectively as a single memory" — Communications Biology**
  - Investigated whether multiple memories can be reactivated concurrently.
  - Relevant to scalability because a practical TMR system may need to manage many memories and cues rather than isolated laboratory associations.
  - DOI: https://doi.org/10.1038/s42003-020-01512-0

### Recent Work

- **Schechtman, Heilberg & Paller (2023) — "Memory consolidation during sleep involves context reinstatement in humans" — Cell Reports**
  - Showed that cueing individual memories during sleep can influence other uncued memories belonging to the same learned context.
  - Sigma/spindle-related activity was associated with contextual effects.
  - Important because it suggests that the unit affected by TMR may not always be an isolated cue-memory pair.
  - DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.celrep.2023.112331

- **Lazarus & Schechtman (2025) — "Manipulating memory processing during sleep to explore the critical duration of reactivation events" — Neuropsychologia**
  - Investigated the temporal duration of memory-reactivation processes during sleep.
  - Relevant because cue duration and temporal structure may represent scientific variables rather than mere implementation details.
  - DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuropsychologia.2025.109211

- **Subramanian, Zelano, Paller & Schechtman (2025/2026) — "Concurrent Presentation of Memory-Related Odours and Sounds Nullified Sleep Reactivation Benefits" — Journal of Sleep Research**
  - Tested concurrent memory-related olfactory and auditory cueing.
  - The expected TMR benefit was not observed when the two memory-related cue streams were presented concurrently.
  - Relevant to designing multisensory or concurrent cueing paradigms and understanding possible competition between reactivation processes.
  - DOI: https://doi.org/10.1111/jsr.70314

- **Narayan et al. (2026) — "Odor-Cued Targeted Reactivation Was Unable to Selectively Benefit Declarative Memories During Sleep" — Journal of Sleep Research**
  - Tested whether odor cueing could selectively benefit one learned memory context.
  - The paradigm did not produce the intended selective behavioral advantage.
  - Relevant to the limits of assuming that every cue will precisely isolate the intended memory representation.

## Relevance to Neuro-TMR

Schechtman's work challenges a potential simplification in the current reference architecture.

Our pathway primarily describes:

> EEG → sleep-state estimation → cue scheduling → cue delivery → arousal monitoring

This is necessary for intervention control, but it does not guarantee that:

- the intended memory was reactivated;
- only the intended memory was affected;
- contextual memories were not simultaneously reinstated;
- multiple cue streams did not interfere;
- the temporal characteristics of reactivation were appropriate.

His work therefore raises an important longer-term question:

> Should a mature closed-loop TMR system eventually close the loop around memory reactivation itself, rather than only around sleep state?

He is especially useful for distinguishing **successful cue delivery** from **successful memory-specific intervention**.

## Phase-I Claims They Can Challenge

- **TMR can be modeled primarily as selective reactivation of individual cue-associated memories.**
  - His context-reinstatement work shows that cueing one memory can influence contextually related uncued information.

- **Correct sleep-state detection plus appropriately scheduled cue delivery is sufficient to characterize the core closed-loop problem.**
  - His work raises the possibility that confirmation of memory-related processing may eventually matter separately from confirmation of sleep state.

- **Cue scheduling can scale straightforwardly from isolated associations to multiple memories.**
  - His work on multiple-memory reactivation and concurrent sensory cueing can help determine where scalability creates interactions or interference.

- **Cue timing parameters are primarily engineering details.**
  - His recent work on the duration of reactivation events suggests that temporal characteristics may have direct scientific significance.

- **A cue can be assumed to engage the intended memory whenever it is delivered during an appropriate sleep state.**
  - Recent context and odor-cue findings make this assumption worth testing rather than taking for granted.
